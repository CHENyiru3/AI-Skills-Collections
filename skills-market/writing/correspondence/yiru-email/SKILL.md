# Write email in Yiru’s voice

Start with the person opening the message. What do they already know, and what does Yiru want them to understand, decide, or do? Read the latest message and identify who wrote each part of the thread. Use Yiru’s own draft and stated preferences as the strongest evidence of tone. Incoming mail tells you what to answer and how the participants address each other.

## Draft from notes or reply to a thread

- Answer a direct question early. If a time works, confirm it in the first line. A confirmation can be the whole reply. If one logistical detail is missing, ask for it in the next sentence.
- Give a new recipient the shortest introduction that makes the request intelligible. In a follow-up, identify the shared form, meeting, or decision and report what has changed. A new administrator joining a thread may need one sentence explaining what a collaborator already knows.
- Make thanks specific when the source gives you something to acknowledge. After a research discussion, Yiru often names a question worth reconsidering after the conversation. If Yiru only wants to acknowledge two papers, a warm thank-you and a sentence about his interest are enough.
- Ask for the next action in ordinary language. “Could you let me know how to get access?” suits an administrator. “Would you have time to discuss this next week?” suits a meeting request when Yiru has supplied that timeframe. When something may have gone missing, describe what Yiru observed. “I don’t think I received the invitation. Could you resend it?”
- Use the names for projects, methods, and forms that the recipient recognizes. Give a brief reason when it helps them answer. Preserve uncertainty and the difference between interest, an offer, and a commitment. Check names, attachments, dates, and promises against the material Yiru supplied.

## Match the relationship

Follow the greeting already established in the thread. “Hi [first name],” fits a familiar colleague. “Dear Prof. [surname],” is a good first-contact greeting for a professor with a known title. Address an unfamiliar office by its team name if no individual is named. On a group thread, make clear who can answer the request and who is being thanked.

Use conversational first-person English and let warmth show in a concrete phrase such as “Thank you for making time” or “I’d be glad to learn more.” An occasional exclamation mark fits good news or genuine thanks. Keep apologies brief when Yiru wants to acknowledge a delay. Keep any offer of flexibility within the availability Yiru gave. Preserve the sign-off in the draft. Common choices are “Best,” or “Best regards,” followed by “Yiru,” with “Yiru Chen” useful for an introduction.

## Edit at the level requested

For a light polish, keep Yiru’s content, order, level of warmth, and any wording that already works. Fix the particular grammar, idiom, or ambiguity that gets in the reader’s way. If asked to change one line, edit that line only. If asked for feedback only, give feedback. For a broader rewrite, rearrange the material as needed and preserve the meaning and strength of each claim.

For Chinese notes requesting an English reply, carry over the intended relationship, stance, and request in idiomatic English. In every language, distinguish the draft from quoted correspondence and leave the quoted history intact. If the recipient needs a fact that Yiru has not supplied, use a small placeholder or ask for that fact if it determines the reply.

Return copy-ready text in the requested language and format. A new email can use a short, specific subject. For a reply, keep the thread subject and provide just the new message unless Yiru asks for more. For another external format, use that format’s normal opening and closing.
